Fixing Anterior Pelvic Tilt: The Lower Back Pain Corrective Routine That Actually Works

Coach Rachel Stone's no-BS corrective exercise routine for anterior pelvic tilt and lower back pain. Real exercises, real results.

Photo: Tima Miroshnichenko
By Rachel Stone · June 07, 2026 · Fitness & Training · 5 min read

Anterior pelvic tilt is probably causing your lower back pain. Not your mattress, not your age, not your genetics. Your pelvis is tipped forward, your hip flexors are strangling your lumbar spine, and your glutes have essentially gone on strike. I've seen this pattern in somewhere around 80% of the desk workers and recreational athletes who walk into my sessions complaining of chronic lower back tightness. I remember one client in particular, a competitive cyclist I started working with in early 2022, who had spent close to $1,200 on foam rollers, massage guns, and three rounds of physical therapy without anyone once looking at how his pelvis sat in a standing position. Six weeks of targeted corrective work and the pain was mostly gone. Six weeks.

Here's my editorial position, and I'm not walking it back: most lower back pain programs fail because they treat the symptom, not the postural dysfunction driving it. Stretching your back when your hip flexors are the problem is like mopping the floor while the tap is still running. A 2018 review published in the Journal of Physical Therapy Science found that hip flexor tightness and reduced glute activation were consistently associated with increased lumbar lordosis and lower back pain in sedentary populations. That's the mechanism. That's what we're fixing.

Why Anterior Pelvic Tilt Wrecks Your Lower Back (And Why Stretching Alone Won't Fix It)

When your pelvis tilts forward, your lumbar spine goes into excessive extension. That compresses the posterior joints of the spine and shortens the erector spinae, which are already working overtime to hold you upright. Meanwhile, your hip flexors, specifically the iliopsoas and rectus femoris, are chronically shortened and pulling your pelvis down in the front. Your abdominals are lengthened and weak. Your glutes are inhibited. This is called lower crossed syndrome, a term coined by Dr. Vladimir Janda, and it's not a diagnosis you need an MRI to identify. You can spot it by looking at someone standing sideways.

The mistake most people make is going straight to spinal stretches. Cat-cow, child's pose, lumbar rotations. These feel good in the moment, and I won't tell you to stop doing them entirely, but they're addressing the effect, not the cause. If you don't lengthen the hip flexors and reactivate the glutes, you'll be stretching that lower back every morning for the rest of your life. And here's the thing... the tighter your hip flexors get, the harder your glutes work to compensate, and the less effective they become. It becomes a cycle.

The corrective approach has to be sequenced correctly: inhibit what's overactive, lengthen what's short, activate what's dormant, then integrate the whole pattern under load. Skip any step and you'll get partial results at best.

The Inhibition and Lengthening Phase: Where Most Routines Start Too Late

Before you strengthen anything, you need to turn down the noise from the overactive muscles. For anterior pelvic tilt, that means the hip flexors and the lumbar erectors. Self-myofascial release using a foam roller on the thoracic spine and a targeted ball release on the TFL and rectus femoris takes about 5 minutes and genuinely changes the quality of the stretches that follow. I've tested this both ways with clients, pre-release versus straight to stretching, and the difference in hip flexor length during a Thomas test position is noticeable every single time.

After foam rolling, you move into static stretching. The 90/90 hip flexor stretch is my go-to, held for 30 to 60 seconds per side with a posterior pelvic tilt actively maintained throughout. This is critical: if you let your lower back arch while stretching your hip flexor, you're not actually lengthening the iliopsoas. You're just hanging off your lumbar spine. Squeeze your glute on the stretched side, tuck your pelvis under slightly, and then lean forward. That's the actual stretch.

Add a kneeling hip flexor stretch with a reach overhead to bias the rectus femoris, and a couch stretch if you're dealing with severe tightness. Three sets of each, 30 seconds minimum. This isn't glamorous work. But it's the work that actually changes posture over time.

Glute Activation and Core Bracing: The Part Nobody Does Correctly

Glute activation sounds basic. It is not basic. In my work with competitive runners and cyclists, I've found that a significant number of athletes who can squat their bodyweight still have near-zero glute activation during gait. The muscle is present. It just doesn't fire when it should. Research published in the International Journal of Sports Physical Therapy (2018) confirmed that targeted glute activation protocols significantly improved gluteus maximus EMG activity during functional tasks compared to general strength training alone. Targeting matters.

Start with supine glute bridges, but do them right. Feet flat, hip-width apart, drive through the heels, and hold the top position for a full 2-second squeeze before lowering. Three sets of 15. Then move to clamshells with a light resistance band just above the knees, 3 sets of 20 per side, focusing on not letting your pelvis rock. And here's where I lose people: the dead bug. Not because it's a glute exercise, but because it trains your deep core to brace your spine while your limbs are moving. That's the actual function of your abs. Not crunches. Spinal stability under load.

I'm not 100% sure this works for everyone at the same pace, and honestly, my friend Sara tried this exact sequence for about four weeks and told me she felt zero difference, which was mildly embarrassing because I'd sent her the routine myself. Turned out she was extending her lower back every single time she did a bridge, which completely negates the point. A video check-in fixed it in one session. The exercises work, but the execution has to be right. Sometimes you need a second set of eyes.

The Integration Phase: Loading the Pattern and Making It Stick

Corrective exercise without integration is a dead end. You can't just do clamshells forever and expect your body to move differently under load. Once your hip flexors are consistently more mobile and your glutes are firing reliably in isolation, you need to train the full pattern. That means compound movements performed with strict pelvic position. The goblet squat is your best friend here, because holding a weight in front of your chest actively encourages an upright torso and makes it very hard to fall into anterior tilt. Start with 12 to 16 kilograms, 3 sets of 10, and focus obsessively on keeping your pelvis neutral throughout.

Single-leg Romanian deadlifts are next. They challenge hip hinge mechanics, force the glute to stabilize, and expose any asymmetry between sides. If you've been living with anterior pelvic tilt for a long time, one side is almost certainly worse. Train them separately. From a periodization standpoint, if you want to understand how to progress these exercises over time without stalling, the principles behind linear vs undulating vs block periodization models apply directly to corrective loading phases, and they're worth understanding before you start adding weight.

The final piece is posture awareness during the rest of your day. There's no corrective routine strong enough to overcome 8 hours of sitting with your pelvis dumped forward. Set a timer every 45 minutes. Stand up. Perform a posterior pelvic tilt. Squeeze your glutes for 5 seconds. Sit back down with intention. It sounds trivial. The CDC's ergonomics research consistently links prolonged static postures to musculoskeletal dysfunction. You cannot exercise your way out of a posture problem if the posture never changes outside the gym.

  • Foam roll TFL and thoracic spine: 5 minutes before every session
  • 90/90 hip flexor stretch: 3 x 60 seconds per side, pelvis tucked
  • Supine glute bridge with 2-second hold: 3 x 15
  • Clamshell with band: 3 x 20 per side
  • Dead bug: 3 x 10 per side, slow and controlled
  • Goblet squat: 3 x 10, neutral pelvis throughout
  • Single-leg RDL: 3 x 8 per side, build gradually

Run this routine 3 times per week for a minimum of 8 weeks before judging the results. Most people quit at week 3. That's exactly why most people still have lower back pain.

Today, before you do anything else, do a standing posture check in front of a mirror. Stand naturally. Look sideways. If your belly tips forward and your lower back curves sharply inward, you've confirmed the problem. Then spend 10 minutes on the hip flexor stretch and glute bridge routine above. Not tomorrow. Today. If you're new to structured training and the gym feels like a barrier to even starting this, the honest advice for managing gym anxiety as a beginner is a practical read before you commit to showing up consistently. But the corrective work? You can start that on your living room floor right now.
