**Title:** Why does adding more cardio barely move the scale for so many people?

Something I wish I had understood years earlier: piling on cardio is one of the least efficient ways to lose fat. That is not because cardio is useless.

Two things happen when you add a big block of daily cardio. First, your body partly adjusts its total energy budget. Research on constrained energy expenditure suggests that past a certain activity level, your total daily burn rises less than the exercise numbers imply, because other processes quietly get trimmed. Second, appetite climbs. Most people eat back a good chunk of what they burned without noticing. A handful of something here, a bigger dinner there.

I worked with a former college rower who logged over 300 minutes on the erg in a month and lost 1.4 pounds. Her training was not the problem. Her food intake and her lack of strength work were.

What actually moved things for her was a modest, consistent calorie deficit, lifting three times a week to hold onto muscle, and treating cardio as a health tool rather than the fat loss engine.

## QUORA
**Answer this question:** Why am I not losing weight even though I do an hour of cardio every day?

Short answer: an hour of daily cardio burns less net energy than your watch suggests, and most people unknowingly eat back much of what is left.

This is one of the most common frustrations I hear from clients. Here is what is usually going on.

1. Your body adapts its energy budget. Research on constrained energy expenditure (Herman Pontzer's work is a good starting point) suggests that total daily calorie burn does not rise in a straight line with activity. When exercise goes up, the body tends to trim energy spent elsewhere, so 500 calories on the screen may be a lot less in real terms.

2. Trackers overestimate. Treadmill and wrist estimates are often generous, sometimes by a wide margin.

3. Appetite compensates. Hard cardio makes most people hungrier: a slightly bigger lunch, a post-workout snack, an extra glass of juice. None of it feels like much, but together it can erase the deficit.

4. Everyday movement drops. After a tough session, people tend to sit more for the rest of the day without noticing.

I saw this clearly with a former college rower I trained who put in over 300 minutes on the rowing machine in a month and lost 1.4 pounds. Once we tracked her food honestly and added strength work, the scale finally moved. I wrote up her full story here if you want more detail: https://siavashsed.github.io/fitpulsepro/why-cardio-alone-fails-fat-loss/

What I would change for you:
- Set a modest calorie deficit and track your intake for two weeks to see the real picture.
- Lift weights two to four times a week so you keep muscle while you lose fat.
- Keep cardio for health and conditioning, not as your main fat loss tool.
- Prioritize protein and sleep, since both help control appetite.

Cardio is great for you. It just rarely works on its own.
