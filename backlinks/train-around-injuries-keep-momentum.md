**Title:** Anyone else learn that an injury doesn't mean stopping training, just stopping loading the injured tissue?

I've coached competitive athletes for about ten years, and the biggest mindset shift I've seen is this: when someone gets hurt, the default is to shut everything down. Rest completely, wait for it to heal, start over.

But most injuries are local. A sprinter I coach tweaked her hamstring eight weeks out from conference. She wanted a full month off. Instead we kept her upper body strength work going and put her on a 30-minute bike session the next day, while the hamstring got only the loading it could actually tolerate.

A few things that have held up for me:

1. Separate the injured tissue from everything else. Only one area needs to change.
2. Keep the training habit. Showing up matters as much as the workout itself, especially mentally.
3. Swap the movement, not the effort. Bike, upper body, and core work can keep your conditioning from falling off a cliff.
4. Reload gradually instead of jumping back to last week's numbers.

A full stop tends to cost more than the injury did, because you lose fitness and rhythm and then re-injure yourself rushing back.

Curious how others here have handled it. Did you keep training through an injury, or take full time off? What worked?

## QUORA
**Answer this question:** Should I stop working out completely when I get injured?

In most cases, no. I've coached competitive athletes for ten years, and the most common mistake I see is treating an injury like a pause button. The better approach is to stop loading the injured tissue the way you did before, and keep everything else moving.

Here's how I think about it.

First, figure out what is actually injured. A pulled hamstring doesn't stop your upper body, your core, or your cardiovascular system from training. A sore shoulder doesn't stop you from working your legs. Most injuries are local, so your response should be local too.

Second, replace rather than remove. If running aggravates a leg injury, a stationary bike session or upper body work can maintain conditioning and strength. Keep the effort honest, change the movement.

Third, protect the habit. Showing up to train, even in a modified way, keeps your routine and your identity as someone who trains. Athletes who take long full breaks often struggle more with motivation than with the injury itself.

Fourth, reload gradually. When the injured area is ready, bring it back at a reduced load and build up over a couple of weeks instead of jumping to where you left off. Rushing back is the most common way people re-injure themselves.

One important caveat: if you have severe pain, swelling, numbness, or a suspected fracture or tear, get it assessed by a physician or physical therapist before training around it. Guidance should be specific to your injury.
