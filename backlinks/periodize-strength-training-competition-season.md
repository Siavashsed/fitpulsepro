**Title:** Anyone else keep lifting heavy into competition season and watch their athletes get slower?

I coached a 400m sprinter a few years back who hit a 405-pound back squat in March, three weeks into her outdoor season. I was thrilled. So I kept her lifting heavy twice a week, because the numbers were great and I didn't want to lose them.

Her times stalled, then got worse. Her legs were tired every session, and the heavy work was eating the recovery she needed for track work. The strength was real. It just wasn't the thing limiting her anymore.

What I do now is plan backward from the biggest meet of the year. Heavy strength work goes early, when there's plenty of runway. As the date gets closer, the focus shifts to power and speed: jumps, lighter fast lifts, sprint work. Once the season starts, lifting drops to short maintenance sessions that keep the strength without taxing the legs.

The hard part is emotional. Watching a big lift number sit still feels like losing ground, but it's usually the right call.

Curious how others handle this, especially if you coach or compete in more than one season a year. Do you taper lifting by the calendar or by how the athlete feels?

## QUORA
**Answer this question:** How should I periodize my strength training around a competition season?

Start with the date that matters most and count backward. Most people plan forward from today, which tends to produce an athlete who peaks in the gym and arrives at the meet tired.

Here's the structure I use with competitive athletes.

Phase one, the furthest from competition: heavy strength work. This is when you build the base, with squats, deadlifts, presses and pulls at low reps and high loads. You have time to recover and time to absorb it.

Phase two, as the date closes in: shift to power and speed. The weights come down and the intent goes up. Jumps, medicine ball throws, fast lifts and sport-specific speed work replace the grinding sets. You're converting strength into something you can actually use.

Phase three, once the season starts: short maintenance sessions. One or two brief lifts that keep your strength from fading without leaving your legs heavy for training and competition.

The mistake I made early in my career is skipping phase three. In 2019 a 400m sprinter I coached squatted 405 pounds in March, three weeks into her outdoor season. I was so pleased with the number that I kept her lifting heavy twice a week. Her track performance suffered because the lifting was eating her recovery. Strength wasn't her limiter anymore, fatigue was.

A few practical rules:

1. Pick one primary competition, even if you have several.
2. Write the phases on a calendar with dates, working backward.
3. Cut volume before you cut intensity when maintaining.
4. Judge progress by performance in your sport, not your gym numbers.
