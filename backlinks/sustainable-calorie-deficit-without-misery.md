## QUORA
**Answer this question:** Why do crash diets fail, and what is a sustainable calorie deficit?

Crash diets fail because the deficit is too big to maintain. They usually work for about three weeks, and then the body and the brain push back hard.

I've coached competitive athletes for ten years, and I've watched it happen many times. When you cut calories aggressively, several things happen at once. Hunger rises and food starts taking up most of your mental space. Energy drops, so workouts get worse. Sleep often suffers. Eventually willpower runs out, and one slip turns into a full rebound. The scale may move quickly at first, but a lot of that early drop isn't what you wanted to lose, and the way you got it is hard to repeat.

A sustainable deficit is much smaller than most people expect: roughly 10 to 20 percent below what you burn each day. Here is what I tell clients.

1. Estimate maintenance, then subtract only 10 to 20 percent. If you burn 2,500 calories, that means eating roughly 2,000 to 2,250.

2. Prioritize protein. Enough protein helps preserve muscle while you lose fat, and it keeps you fuller.

3. Eat food you actually enjoy. A plan you hate is a plan you will quit. Build meals around foods you like and can repeat.

4. Be patient. A smaller deficit means slower weight loss, but you keep your training quality, your mood, and your consistency, and consistency is what produces results over months.

I once put a sprinter on 1,400 calories to drop six pounds before a meet. She lost the weight in two weeks, but the cost to her training and her wellbeing showed me that faster isn't better.
