**Title:** Anyone else spend years doing sets of 12 on everything because "that's the hypertrophy range"?

I coached a collegiate rower a few years back who'd done sets of 12 on every lift for two years. Same weights, same rep target, early morning sessions. Her progress had basically flatlined, and she was convinced she was doing everything right because 8 to 12 is what every magazine says.

What clicked for both of us is that the 8 to 12 window isn't a magic zone. The research on hypertrophy shows muscle growth happens across a wide range, roughly 6 to 30 reps, as long as sets are taken close to failure. Effort and proximity to failure matter far more than hitting a specific number.

The rough framework I use now:

- 1 to 5 reps: best for building maximal strength
- 6 to 30 reps near failure: size
- 15+ reps: local muscular endurance

So if you've been stuck on 3x12 forever, the problem probably isn't the rep count. It's that the sets stop well short of hard, or that you never vary the stimulus when your goals shift.

Curious what rep ranges others here actually run, and whether you've noticed a difference moving outside the standard 8 to 12.

## QUORA
**Answer this question:** What is the best rep range for building muscle, strength, and endurance?

After ten years coaching competitive athletes, the short answer is this: choose your rep range based on the quality you want most, and then train hard enough for it to matter.

For maximal strength, work in the 1 to 5 rep range. Heavy loads train your nervous system to recruit more muscle fibers and produce force efficiently. This is why powerlifters spend most of their time here.

For muscle size, the commonly quoted 8 to 12 range is far too narrow. Research consistently shows that hypertrophy can occur anywhere from roughly 6 to 30 reps, provided the sets are taken close to failure. What drives growth is mechanical tension and effort, not a magic number. A set of 25 that ends two reps from failure can build as much muscle as a set of 10 that does.

For local muscular endurance, think 15 reps and above. Higher reps and shorter rest build the muscle's ability to resist fatigue, which matters for sports like rowing, cycling, and combat sports.

Here's where most people get tripped up: they live in the middle and never push sets hard. I once coached a collegiate rower who did sets of 12 on everything for two years. Her reps were fine. Her effort was comfortable, and her results reflected that. Once we shifted some work heavier, some lighter, and pushed sets closer to failure, she started progressing again within weeks.

Practical takeaways:

1. Use heavy low-rep work for your main lifts if strength is a priority.
2. Use a mix of moderate and high reps, near failure, for size.
3. Add higher-rep finishers for endurance and joint-friendly volume.
4. Track your effort, not just your numbers.
