24 Hour Fitness Just Filed for Bankruptcy. The Gym Model Was Already Dying.

24 Hour Fitness and Gold's Gym filed bankruptcy in 2020 as gyms shut down. A trainer breaks down what the collapse means for how you train.

Photo: AJ Ahamad
By Rachel Stone · April 15, 2020 · Fitness & Training · 4 min read

If you woke up this June to find your 24 Hour Fitness locked and a bankruptcy notice taped to the door, you're not imagining a one-off. The chain filed Chapter 11 on June 15 and is shuttering more than 100 clubs, and it's the third major gym operator to fold this year. I've coached out of six different commercial gyms over the past decade, and I texted a client the morning the news broke because we lost the exact squat rack we'd built her whole meet prep around. She'd been paying $44 a month for eighteen months. Now that money, and that rack, are just gone.

Here's my read as someone who has watched this business from the inside: COVID didn't kill these companies. It exposed how thin the margins already were. The all-you-can-eat, sign-them-up-and-hope-they-don't-show model was fragile before anyone had heard of a lockdown. 2020 just yanked the tablecloth.

The year three gym giants went down in six months

Start with the timeline, because it's blunt. Gold's Gym filed for bankruptcy in early May, closing around 30 company-owned locations. 24 Hour Fitness followed in June, listing between $1 billion and $10 billion in liabilities and planning to close roughly 130 clubs, per Reuters. Town Sports International, the parent of New York Sports Clubs, was circling the drain and would file in September.

These aren't boutique studios that never had a plan. 24 Hour Fitness ran about 400 clubs and something like 3.4 million members. When a company that size can't survive a few months of closed doors, the problem was never just the closed doors. Their debt load was already crushing, and the government-ordered shutdowns turned a slow bleed into a hemorrhage.

And the fallout keeps spreading. The IHRSA, the industry's own trade group, has been warning that a huge share of health clubs could close permanently without relief. That's not a doomsayer talking. That's the gyms' own lobbyists.

Why the all-access model was rotten before COVID

Let me tell you how the sausage got made, because I saw it up close. The big-box economics run on the no-show. A club sells far more memberships than its floor could ever physically hold, betting that most people quit coming by March. The $30-a-month member who never swipes in is the most profitable customer they have.

That model only works if people keep paying while staying home. The second a pandemic gives millions of those ghost members a legitimate reason to cancel, or freeze, or dispute the charge, the whole thing wobbles. And that's exactly what happened this spring. Cancellations spiked, dues collection cratered, and companies that had leveraged themselves to the ceiling on long commercial leases had no cushion.

In my work with competitive lifters, I've watched these clubs cut the things that actually build strong bodies first. The platform gets replaced with three more cardio machines. The chalk gets banned. Staff trainers get pushed toward selling supplements over programming. When your business depends on people not using the gym, you stop investing in the gym. This year the bill came due.

Where the members actually went

They didn't stop training. They moved it home. Peloton reported its fiscal first-quarter revenue jumped around 172 percent year over year, and demand outran what the company could even ship, according to its own investor filings. Kettlebells and adjustable dumbbells were sold out for months. If you tried to buy a squat rack online in April, you know.

I'll be honest, I didn't see the home boom coming this hard, and I got one thing wrong early. My friend Dana, who I'd nagged for years about her form, bought a cheap resistance-band kit and one of those door anchors in March because every rack in the state was backordered. I quietly assumed she'd quit by May. She did not. She's arguably in better shape now than when she had a $60 membership she used twice a week, mostly because she stopped commuting forty minutes to sit on her phone between sets. So... maybe I was the problem. The full story of that surge is worth reading in what Peloton's 2020 explosion really revealed about how we train.

The catch nobody advertises: home training exposes whether you actually know what you're doing. No trainer walking the floor. No social pressure. Just you and a plan, or you and a shrug.

What this means for how you should train now

My editorial position is simple. Stop treating a gym membership as a fitness plan. It never was one. A building full of equipment you don't program around is just an expensive place to feel guilty.

The lifters and athletes I coach who barely noticed 2020 had two things in common. They understood progressive overload well enough to apply it with whatever load was in front of them, and they had a written program they could run in a garage, a park, or a hotel room. That's the durable skill. The chrome and the mirrors were never it.

Now the honest complexity, because I hate one-size answers. Home training is not better for everyone. Some people genuinely need the accountability of showing up somewhere, and some lifts, heavy barbell work in particular, are hard to replicate with bands and a bench. If you're chasing a real squat or deadlift number, a rack still matters. The mistake is assuming the building supplies the discipline. It doesn't. You do. And your recovery matters as much as your training, which is why I keep pointing clients toward why seven hours of sleep beats two-a-day sessions.

Today, before you renew anything, pull up your last three months of gym visits. Most apps track it. If you went fewer than eight times a month, cancel and put that money toward a set of adjustable dumbbells, a pull-up bar, and one month with a coach who'll write you an actual program. You'll train more, not less, and no bankruptcy filing can lock you out of your own living room.

Frequently asked questions

Why did 24 Hour Fitness file for bankruptcy in 2020?

The chain filed Chapter 11 in June 2020 after months of pandemic closures wiped out membership dues while it carried heavy debt and long-term leases. The shutdowns didn't create the weakness, they exposed a model that already ran on thin margins and no-show members.

Will I lose my membership money if my gym goes bankrupt?

Often yes, at least the prepaid or annual portion. In a Chapter 11 filing, members become unsecured creditors near the back of the line. Dispute any charges for months you couldn't access the club, and check your card issuer's chargeback window.

Is home workout gear actually as effective as a gym?

For most general fitness goals, yes, if you follow a real program with progressive overload. The gap shows up in heavy barbell work, where a squat rack and loaded bar are hard to replace with bands or light dumbbells.
